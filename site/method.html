How the study works

Three textbook ways to trade pairs, run side by side under one set of rules that was fixed before any result was seen, with realistic costs and statistics that correct for luck.

The question

Pair trading buys one stock and sells a similar one when their prices drift unusually far apart, and unwinds when they come back together. It made money in US stocks in the 1960s–1990s (Gatev, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst, 2006) but later studies found the profits shrinking (Do and Faff, 2010). This study asks two things for euro-area large caps since 2004: does pair trading still make money after realistic costs, and which of the three classic ways of choosing and hedging pairs holds up best?

Data

Daily closing prices adjusted for splits and dividends, from Yahoo Finance, for … large caps listed in euros (Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Belgium, Finland, Austria, Portugal and Ireland), from 2003. Staying in one currency keeps exchange rates out of every pair. Pairs are only formed between stocks in the same sector, which gives … candidate pairs.

Free price data has errors, and a pair strategy is very sensitive to them: one bad print looks like a huge spread and a guaranteed profit. The cleaning rules, applied in this order:

  1. Non-positive prices, left by a broken dividend back-adjustment (Crédit Agricole before 2004, AB InBev before 2008), are treated as missing.
  2. Days when most markets were shut are dropped; gaps of up to five days (local holidays) are filled with the last price.
  3. A jump of more than 25% that returns to within 10% of its starting level inside five days is a bad print, and is removed.
  4. The confirmed errors below are fixed by hand: a single date has its jump removed by rescaling the earlier history; a range is removed.

Real crashes stay in. Fortis losing three quarters of its value in October 2008 or the Irish banks in 2009 are exactly what a pair trader had to live through; removing them would flatter the results.

The universe

The backtest protocol

Every month a new cohort starts. It looks back over the past … trading days (about a year, the formation period) to pick its … best pairs, then trades them for the next … days (about six months, the trading period). So six cohorts are always running side by side, and the portfolio return is their average, as in Gatev et al. A pair can only be chosen from data that ended before it trades: every result is out of sample.

  • Signal. Each method turns the pair into a z-score: how many standard deviations the spread is from where it should be.
  • Entry and exit. Open when the z-score goes beyond ±…: sell the expensive stock and buy the cheap one. Close when the z-score crosses …. Anything still open when the six months end is closed then, win or lose.
  • Execution. A signal seen at one day's close is traded at the next day's close. This removes the bid-ask bounce that flatters same-day execution.
  • Sizing. Each pair gets one unit of capital: one unit long and one unit short, split by the hedge ratio. The cohort's return is total pair profit divided by the number of pairs it was meant to hold, whether or not they traded (the "committed capital" return, the conservative choice).
  • Trading costs, stock by stock. Every purchase and sale pays a commission of … plus half the bid-ask spread. Free data has no quotes, so the spread is modelled: proportional to each stock's volatility over the past month, scaled so the typical stock in calm years (2015–2019) pays …. Spreads therefore widen in a crisis, when pair trades cluster. (The Abdi–Ranaldo estimator from daily highs and lows was tried first and rejected: on these liquid stocks it returned zero for almost half the stock-days.)
  • Transaction taxes on every purchase, including buying back a stock sold short, by the country where the company is registered (so Airbus and STMicroelectronics, Dutch companies listed in Paris, pay no French tax). The rates are listed below.
  • Short-selling bans. While a regulator banned new short positions, the strategy cannot open a trade that sells that stock short; it waits, and trades if the signal is still there when the ban lifts. Positions already open may stay, as the rules allowed. Bans that only stopped naked short selling (selling without borrowing first, as in Germany in 2008) do not affect a strategy that borrows. The bans used are listed below.
  • Borrowing stock to sell short costs … a year of the short position. The capital set aside earns the overnight rate (below).
  • Capacity. How large could the strategy be? For each trade of the last three years, the portfolio size at which it would take … of either stock's average daily trading (home exchange only, so a cautious figure); the published capacity is the size at which 95% of trades fit.

The three methods

1. Distance (Gatev, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst, 2006)

Rebase both prices to 1 at the start of formation and pick the pairs whose paths are closest: the smallest sum of squared differences.

SSD = Σt (p̃A,t − p̃B,t)²,   p̃t = Pt / P0

In trading, prices are rebased again at the start and the spread p̃A − p̃B is divided by its formation standard deviation. The hedge is one euro long for one euro short. Simple and model-free, but nothing guarantees that two stocks that moved together will keep doing so.

2. Cointegration (Engle and Granger, 1987; Vidyamurthy, 2004)

Regress one log price on the other over the formation period, and test whether what is left over keeps returning to zero:

log PA,t = α + β log PB,t + εt    then    Δεt = ρ εt−1 + γ Δεt−1 + ut

A pair qualifies when the Engle–Granger test rejects "no cointegration" at the … level (MacKinnon 2010 p-values for two variables), the hedge ratio β is positive, and the half-life of the spread, ln 2 / −ln(1 + ρ), is between one day and the length of the trading period. The most significant pairs are kept. The z-score is εt divided by its formation standard deviation, and the hedge puts β euros on stock B for every euro on stock A.

3. Kalman filter hedge (Chan, 2013)

The same pairs as the cointegration method, so the only difference is how the hedge is set. Instead of fixing α and β for six months, a Kalman filter lets them drift as a random walk and updates them every day:

yt = βt xt + αt + vt,   (βt, αt) = (βt−1, αt−1) + wt,   Var(w) = δ / (1 − δ) · I

Here y and x are log prices rebased to zero at the start of formation, δ = …, and the noise of vt is the variance of the formation-period residual. The filter starts from the formation estimates. The z-score is the forecast error divided by its predicted standard deviation, both computed before that day's price is seen. Position size uses the β known on the day the signal fires and is then held fixed.

Statistics

  • Cash. The capital set aside for the pairs earns the euro overnight rate: EONIA until September 2019, €STR after (ECB data). Return a year is compound growth including that interest; return above cash, the Sharpe ratio (mean over standard deviation, times √252), t-statistics and alphas all use the return in excess of cash. The market is treated the same way.
  • t-statistic uses Newey–West standard errors (10 lags), because overlapping cohorts make daily returns autocorrelated.
  • Confidence intervals and tests between methods come from a circular block bootstrap: history is cut into …-day blocks that are redrawn with replacement 2,000 times, all series together so their correlation is kept. The p-value of a Sharpe difference is the share of redraws, centred on zero, at least as extreme as the observed difference.
  • Factor attribution. Excess returns are regressed on seven factors with Newey–West errors: the market (this universe in euros, above cash); Ken French's European size, value, profitability, investment and momentum factors (built in dollars, but as long-short portfolios they are close to currency-neutral); and a within-sector short-term reversal factor built here. Each month it buys the stocks that did worst against their sector over the past month and sells the ones that did best. Pair trading is known to earn much of its return from this effect (Do and Faff, 2010), so this is the test that matters: alpha left after it is what pairs add.
  • Two halves. The sample is cut exactly in half, a rule fixed in advance, and a regression on a second-half dummy tests whether the average return changed.
  • The parameter grid. With the headline settings, a grid was fixed for each method before any result was seen: formation of … days × … pairs × entry at …, … variants in all. The grid is never used to choose the headline. It feeds two measures of overfitting:
    • The deflated Sharpe ratio (Bailey and López de Prado, 2014): the probability that the true Sharpe ratio beats the best you would expect by luck from that many tries, allowing for skew and fat tails. Above 95% is the usual bar.
    • The probability of backtest overfitting (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu, 2017). History is cut into 16 blocks; for each of the 12,870 ways to pick half of them, the variant with the best Sharpe ratio on that half is ranked on the other half. PBO is how often the winner lands in the bottom half. Near 0 means the ranking is stable; near 50% means it is noise.
  • Beta and correlation are measured against the market as above. A working pair strategy should be close to zero on both.

Limitations

  • Survivorship bias. The universe is today's large caps. Firms that were taken over, went bust or shrank out of the index are missing. Pairs whose stocks later failed are exactly the ones that diverge for good, so the backtest is probably too kind.
  • Free data. Yahoo's adjusted prices contain errors. The rules above catch the large ones; small ones remain.
  • Modelled frictions. Spreads are modelled from volatility, not measured from quotes. The borrow fee is flat, while real fees jump for hard-to-borrow stocks and loans can be recalled. Ban dates come from regulators' announcements as reported by law firms and the press; two are approximate and flagged in the table. Belgium's stock-exchange tax is left out because foreign institutions are exempt.
  • Closing prices only. No intraday data, no auctions modelled, no capacity limit.
  • One headline setting. The rules follow the literature and were fixed in advance; the grid measures how fragile they are but is not searched for a better one. A desk would add stop-losses, sector limits and volatility sizing.
  • Simple returns within a cohort. Profits are not reinvested inside a six-month trading period, only between periods.

Reproduce it

Everything is open source. With uv installed:

uv sync
uv run pytest
uv run python -m pipeline

The last command downloads prices, runs every cohort (under a minute), and writes data/results.json, the file this site reads. Use it however you like; please cite the site.
